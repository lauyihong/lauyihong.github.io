Learned skills on top of classical perception

How we built our TOWER policy: five dual-arm block-manipulation tasks, ten variants, a few dozen demonstrations each, three weeks. What broke, how we fixed it, what we tried and dropped, and what the final policy still cannot do.

The Problem

Each policy is a websocket server. Per request it receives four RGB images (two static cameras, two wrist cameras), 16 joint positions, two gripper widths and the task variant, and returns 16 rows of joint commands at 30 Hz.

Tasks

  • T1–T3 tower transfer, cross-tower transfer and vertical stacking: pick and place blocks, two- and three-layer variants.
  • T4 full-tower push-down: push the blocks of a tower out one by one without dragging the blocks below.
  • T5 tower insertion: insert blocks into a tower with about 1 mm of clearance per side, without moving its walls and roof.

Scoring and data

  • Per variant s = 60·LHPR + 30·TSR + 10·P/(1+ODR): progress first, then success, then a penalty for disturbing other blocks.
  • Tasks 4–5 count three times, two thirds of the overall score.
  • Ten official episodes per variant. Training data: the official demonstrations only, about 40–50 per variant.

Samples from the official dataset

Each demonstration is one HDF5 episode recorded at 30 Hz in Isaac Sim: four 640×480 camera streams, joint states and commands, end-effector poses, the initial block poses, a language instruction and the simulator configuration that generated it. How It Evolved

From an end-to-end VLA to a modular learned policy

Each turn had a concrete reason.

Oct 11–12

Pick a backbone

ACT, our own diffusion model and π0.5 with LoRA all lost to a full fine-tune of π0.5. We also set a rule we kept to the end: the environment is fixed, only the policy changes.

Oct 13–16

A replica we can trust

We rebuilt the evaluation simulator in Isaac Sim from the configuration stored in the official HDF5 files. Comparing it with official results exposed that the official Task 1–3 static cameras were rotated by 180° relative to the training data.

Oct 17–23

Precision and multitask π0.5

Averaging eight flow samples (mean8) raised the official Task 3-2 success rate from 0.30 to 0.80 without retraining. A multitask model reached 56.7% success on Tasks 1–3, and 30 Hz interpolation stopped the left arm from dropping blocks.

Oct 23–28

Tasks 4–5 change the plan

π0.5 behaviour cloning was weak on push-down and insertion (0/12 on Task 4 in our replica). A hand-designed program that reuses demonstrations retargeted to the perceived block poses (the RAX program) was strong on all three task families. On Oct 27 the organisers published the scoring formula and banned replay and fixed scripted solutions.

Oct 28–Nov 1

From program to learned policy

In five days we replaced the program's decision and motion parts by learned networks (RAX-AM) and kept the classical perception. Successive versions (v1 → v6d) took it from 77 to 91 successful episodes out of 100, ahead of the program it replaced (90/100).

Final Design

RAX-AM

Each task is a sequence of skills taken from the demonstrations' phase labels (approach, close, lift, transport, place, release, …). Perception measures where the next block is; two small networks decide which skill is active and produce the motion.

Learned

  • Skill selector. From joints, gripper widths, the policy's own command history, time in the skill and the perceived offsets, it predicts the current skill and when it ends.
  • Executor. Conditioned on the skill, its progress and the offsets, it outputs the next 16 command rows as increments.
  • Training. Behaviour cloning on the official training demonstrations, augmented by kinematic retargeting: each skill segment's tool path is moved to a random block offset and re-solved by inverse kinematics, so the networks learn to follow offsets the demonstrations rarely show.

Classical

  • Perception. An edge-based template fit on the two static cameras, once per block, gives the block's offset (±22 mm, ±12°) from a reference pose.
  • Safety filter. Joint and step limits; a rejected chunk is held or slowed, never replaced by stored motion.
  • Task-specific modules. A 45-row start transition on T1-2, and four corrections on T5-3: a height loop, a slowed push window with a height offset, and a slot-corner check.
  • No demonstration rows are stored or replayed at inference.
Problems & Fixes

Most gains came from understanding a failure, not from more training

Numbers marked “replica” come from our local simulator; the others from official evaluations.

The simulator was hidden in the data

Problem

Our first local evaluations ranked models in the wrong order.

Fix

The official HDF5 files carry the simulator configuration (gains, latency, gripper, tolerances). A replica built from it matched the official result of our Task 3-2 model (0.52 vs 0.45) and stayed reliable for Tasks 1–3.

Upside-down cameras

Problem

Models that worked locally failed officially on Tasks 1–3.

Fix

The official static images were rotated by 180° relative to the training data. Rotating them back (camfix) fixed it; every Task 1–3 evaluation before that had been affected.

Good grasps, poor placement

Problem

π0.5 grasped reliably but placed poorly on Task 3-2 (success 0.30).

Fix

Averaging eight flow samples per request (mean8) raised success to 0.80 with the same weights.

The left arm dropped blocks

Problem

Commands at 10 Hz excited the left arm and shook blocks loose.

Fix

Interpolating to 30 Hz removed the drops (replica: 14/21 → 0/21); training on all three 30 Hz phases used the rest of the data.

A start pose no demonstration had

Problem

RAX-AM v1 succeeded on T1-2 in only 2 of 10 episodes.

Fix

The official start differed from the demonstrated start by up to 0.165 rad. A 45-row transition to the demonstrated start gave 10/10.

A safety filter that froze the robot

Problem

After rejecting one oversized step, the policy kept proposing it and never advanced.

Fix

After repeated holds, the chunk is slewed from the last command within the unchanged limits.

Insertion with 1 mm of clearance

Problem

On T5-3 the held block met the walls, and the first push dragged the roof.

Fix

Failure modes found in official videos and reproduced in the replica led to four declared modules; T5-3 went from 5/10 to 8/10 with the same network weights.

A rule change five days before the end

Problem

Our strongest policy, the RAX program, had no learned part when scripted solutions were banned.

Fix

We moved its decisions and motions into learned networks trained on the demonstrations, and declared every classical component that remained.

Limitations

What the final policy still cannot do

Perception

  • Vision is used once per block, from the static cameras only; a block that moves after it was measured is not tracked.
  • The wrist cameras only serve a blank-image check.
  • The template fit is tuned to the simulator's rendering, and its search range (±22 mm, ±12°) bounds what the policy can handle.

Learning

  • Deterministic MLPs imitate near-identical demonstrations and their retargeted copies; generalisation comes mostly from the augmentation and the perception, not from the networks.
  • On Tasks 1–3 the executor builds on its own commands rather than the measured joints, so it is open-loop within a skill.
  • No recovery from states far from the demonstrations, such as a dragged lower block on Task 4.

Engineering

  • T5-3 relies on four classical corrections tuned in our replica; T1-2 needs a scripted start transition.
  • The Task 4–5 replica was never calibrated against official results and ranked our Task 4 models in the opposite order.
  • Ten episodes per variant leave large uncertainty, and our final version choice used official scores.
Lessons
  • Calibrate the evaluator before trusting it. An uncalibrated replica is good for rejecting broken versions, not for close calls.
  • Look for the mechanism. Configuration hidden in the data, a rotated camera, a 10 Hz excitation and an unseen start pose each became a fix.
  • Inference and execution moved the score more than training. Holding after done, sample averaging, 30 Hz interpolation and a start transition each beat a retrain.
  • Lower offline error did not mean better closed-loop behaviour. We saw it repeatedly; offline metrics became gates only.
  • Read the score weights early. Tasks 4–5 were two thirds of the score, and we started training on them too late.
  • Freeze earlier. Our last two versions were still queued at the deadline.
